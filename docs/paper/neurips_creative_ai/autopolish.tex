\begin{abstract}
Aesthetic judgment is plural. The same image can move one viewer and leave another cold, and both reactions are right, because they belong to different people. So the honest answer to ``how good is this image?'' is not one number but the spread of reactions an image draws from a crowd. We ask whether an off-the-shelf vision-language model (VLM) can imitate that spread well enough to guide creative work. We describe a single viewer persona in plain text, such as their age, education, and taste in art, ask the model to react as that person would, and repeat for many viewers to build a synthetic audience. Aggregating those reactions gives a predicted crowd response. Across three datasets of human-rated images we show that only 19-47\% of one person's rating is shared with other viewers, thus an individual score is mostly private taste and cannot be predicted. A group's average, in contrast, is steady enough that a fresh set of viewers reproduces it almost exactly with 0.96 correlation. The simulated crowd also picks up how taste differs between kinds of viewers. When it predicts that one group will like an image more than another group does, real people from those groups tend to agree, at a correlation of $0.17$, while without the viewer personas the correlation is only $0.02$. This carries over to AI-generated image pairs, where the simulated crowd can predict which of two pictures people would prefer, and its accuracy gets better as more viewer personas are added. Building on these findings, we introduce AutoPolish, an auto-research style editing loop where the synthetic audience serves as the creative critic. It asks the simulated crowd to critique an image and turns their plural judgment into an improvement instruction for a language-conditioned image editor. The edited image is then examined by a separate aesthetic judge, which decides how successful the edit was and steers the next round of the loop. We show that AutoPolish measurably improves aesthetic scores while keeping the picture recognizably itself. We further show that the synthetic audience raises $3.1\times$ more distinct objections than a single generic critic. Our implementation is available at \url{https://github.com/<anon>/autopolish}.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Ask ten people to rate a photograph and you will rarely get one answer. A landscape that feels serene to one viewer feels empty to another. An edit that one person calls striking another calls garish. This disagreement is usually treated as noise and averaged away. We treat it as the signal. Taste is plural, and a creative tool that ignores that plurality works to please a viewer who does not exist, the statistical average of everyone, who resembles no one.

Most work on computational aesthetics reduces a picture to a single score, and most creative feedback loops spend their effort raising that one number. We take the honest answer to ``how will this image land?'' to be the whole spread of reactions a picture draws from people: where it centers, how far apart viewers fall, and how it shifts from one kind of viewer to another. The question this paper asks is whether an off-the-shelf vision-language model (VLM) can imitate that spread, and whether the imitation is trustworthy enough to steer a real creative decision.

Our method is deliberately simple and trains nothing. We write one viewer down in plain text, giving their age, education, familiarity with art, personality, and nationality, ask the model to react to an image as that person would, and repeat for many viewers. Averaging their reactions gives the predicted crowd response, and that is the entire method. One measurement then organizes everything that follows. A single person's rating is mostly their own private taste, so very little of it can be predicted by any model at all. The crowd average is a different matter, because the private part of each reaction cancels out in the average and what remains is steady and predictable. This contrast tells us where a synthetic audience can be believed and where it cannot.

We then put the audience to work. AutoPolish is an auto-research style editing loop in which the synthetic audience is the critic. The crowd looks at the current picture, its complaints are gathered into one short instruction, and an image editor carries that instruction out. A separate aesthetic judge decides whether the new picture is better and whether it is still the same picture, and the loop keeps the edit only if it is both. That judge is a different kind of model from the critic and never sees the instruction, so the loop cannot declare its own success. Our contributions are:

\textbf{(1) A plural view of taste, and the measurement that justifies it:} only 19-47\% of one person's rating is shared with other viewers, a group's average is reproduced by a fresh set of viewers at $0.96$ correlation, and a viewer's stated background explains just 1-4\% of their personal taste (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}). \textbf{(2) A synthetic audience, with nothing trained, that reproduces real differences between groups of viewers:} on paintings it matches the observed gaps between groups at $0.17$ correlation, against $0.02$ when the personas are removed, and it improves as the personas get richer (Section~\ref{sec:audience}). \textbf{(3) Evidence that the same averaging works on AI-generated images:} the simulated crowd predicts which of two pictures people prefer, beats a strong majority baseline, and gets more accurate with every persona added (Section~\ref{sec:audience}). \textbf{(4) AutoPolish, an editing loop driven by the audience,} which measurably improves the aesthetic score while keeping the picture recognizably itself, keeps its critic and its judge separate so neither grades its own work, and draws $3.1\times$ more distinct objections than a generic critic (Section~\ref{sec:c4}). We also report in full where the method does not yet work (supplement, Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Simulating people with language models.} A growing literature uses language models as stand-ins for human respondents, from ``silicon sampling'' of survey populations \citep{argyle2023} to persona-conditioned opinion simulation, almost entirely on text. Our audience reacts to \emph{visual} stimuli and does not stop at simulating opinions: we measure when the simulation is faithful and then use it to act. We are also explicit about the level of analysis: prior work chases individual fidelity, which we show is near-impossible in principle, so we relocate the claim to the group.

\paragraph{Personalized and subgroup aesthetics.} Personalized aesthetics assessment predicts a specific user's score from rich per-user attributes \citep{yang2022para}, and reward-based personalization such as PAMELA \citep{pamela2026} tunes a scalar reward to one user. We differ on three axes: we model a \emph{population distribution} rather than one user, we keep every model frozen, and we feed back natural-language critique rather than a scalar. AesBiasBench \citep{aesbiasbench2025} supplies the lens we use to audit subgroup calibration, and work on individual differences \citep{individual2025} motivates our ceiling analysis. We never train a taste predictor.

\paragraph{Feedback loops for generation and editing.} Reward models and preference optimization steer generators toward human-preferred outputs, and instruction-based editors such as FLUX.1-Kontext \citep{flux2024} make targeted edits from text. The recurring hazard is circularity: when the model that proposes an edit also scores it, ``improvement'' can be self-fulfilling. AutoPolish avoids this, because the proposer (the audience critic) and the grader (a held-out aesthetic predictor \citep{schuhmann2022} plus a DINOv2 identity check \citep{oquab2023}) are different model families, and the grader never sees the instruction.


\section{Method}

\paragraph{Persona cards.} A persona card is a short textual description of one real annotator, generated deterministically from the attributes their dataset provides: age band, education, art and photography familiarity, Big-Five scores, and nationality. Attributes a dataset does not record are omitted rather than imputed. A typical card reads \emph{``Viewer profile: 35 to 44, master's degree, high art familiarity, moderate photography experience, nationality: Belgium. Personality (Big-5, 1 to 5): O 4.2, C 3.1, E 2.8, A 3.9, N 2.5.''} The same template is used for the annotators we sample panels from and for the held-out annotators we evaluate on, so our claims are limited to the attributes the card actually contains.

\paragraph{The frozen judge.} The judge is a pretrained vision-language model that we query but never fine-tune. Each query supplies an image, a persona card, and an instruction to answer in a fixed JSON format; the model returns a score, a short rationale, and a few auxiliary fields, parsed as strict JSON with a per-field regular-expression fallback so malformed outputs are recovered rather than dropped. The backbone is \texttt{Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct} \citep{wang2024qwen2vl}, run batched in bf16. The persona-blind control uses an identical prompt with the persona card and the ``as this person'' framing removed.

\paragraph{From a panel to a group prediction.} To predict how a viewer group responds to an image, we sample $N$ persona cards whose attributes match that group, query the judge once per card, and summarize the reactions: the mean and spread of the scores, the most frequent complaints extracted from the rationales, and, for a pair of groups, the difference between their means.

Averaging a panel is not sufficient on its own, because the judge's error is systematic rather than random. Its ratings are compressed toward the middle of the scale: their standard deviation is only 51-58\% of the human standard deviation, 34-56\% of them fall on a single value of the scale, and the endpoints are almost never used (Figure~\ref{fig:style}). Averaging removes variance but leaves this shared compression untouched, which is why raw panel means are less accurate than simply predicting the population mean. We correct it after inference with isotonic regression \citep{niculescu2005}, fitting a monotone mapping from the judge's raw score to a calibrated one by two-fold cross-fitting over images, so every rating is calibrated by a mapping estimated without it. Because the mapping is monotone it changes absolute error but leaves all rankings unchanged, and because it acts on the output the model's weights stay frozen. It also transfers across datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:xcal}), so we fit it once on real images with human ratings and apply it unchanged to generated images, for which no per-image ratings exist to fit on.

\paragraph{AutoPolish.} AutoPolish uses the panel as the critic in an iterative editing loop, under the constraint that the model proposing edits and the model judging them are different, since a loop that scores its own edits can improve its objective without improving the image. Each image is refined for $R=10$ steps of (i) \textbf{critique}: the panel views the current best image, and its complaints are aggregated across personas and accumulated across steps into a single imperative instruction of at most fifteen words; (ii) \textbf{anchored re-edit}: FLUX.1-Kontext-dev \citep{flux2024} applies that instruction to the \emph{original} image rather than the previous edit, so editing artifacts do not compound, producing $K=3$ candidates; (iii) \textbf{held-out scoring}: each candidate is scored by a LAION aesthetic predictor \citep{schuhmann2022}, a CLIP \citep{radford2021} backbone with an MLP head and thus a different model family from the critic, and checked for identity drift with DINOv2 \citep{oquab2023}; (iv) \textbf{accept-if-better}: a candidate replaces the current best only if the aesthetic score improves and DINOv2 similarity to the original stays above $0.78$, so the best-so-far score increases monotonically. We compare four critics: \textsc{static}, a fixed ``improve this image'' instruction; \textsc{blind}, a single persona-free critique; \textsc{society}, ten aggregated personas, which is our method; and \textsc{reward-only}, which optimizes the aesthetic predictor directly and is an upper reference rather than a comparable baseline. Every condition appends the same suffix asking for a visible edit, so prompt wording cannot explain a difference between \textsc{society} and \textsc{blind}, and we deliberately do not score instruction adherence, which would favour whichever condition writes the instruction that is easiest to follow.


\section{Experimental setup}

\paragraph{Data.} We use three human-rated aesthetics datasets that differ in content and, decisively, in how richly their viewers are described. \textbf{PARA} \citep{yang2022para} gives 4{,}000 photographs and $\sim$52k ratings with the richest persona fields (age, gender, education, art and photography experience, Big-Five). \textbf{EVA}, photographs from AVA \citep{murray2012ava}, contributes 4{,}070 images and 137k ratings but thin descriptions (age, gender, region, photographic level). \textbf{LAPIS} contributes 4{,}000 paintings and 90k ratings with nationality, art interest, and age. For generated content we use \textbf{Rapidata} \citep{rapidata2024}: 23{,}445 crowd votes over 898 AI-generated pairs from raters in 120 countries, each vote carrying its rater's country and language. Ratings are normalized to $[0,1]$.

\paragraph{Protocol and metrics.} The primary regime is \emph{cold start}: we hold out a disjoint 20\% of \emph{users} and predict for a viewer the model has no data on, from the persona card alone, adding image-disjoint and, for paintings, artist-disjoint folds for leakage control. Our decisive metric is \textbf{between-group separation}, the correlation between predicted and observed slice-to-slice gaps on the same image, against a persona-blind control whose expected value is zero. We also report group error against a population-mean prior, the panel-size curve, crowd agreement, and, for AutoPolish, held-out gain, identity drift, and complaint diversity. Every headline number carries a 1000-sample bootstrap 95\% confidence interval, clustered by rater and image where both apply, with Holm correction across the slice sweep; a result counts only if its interval excludes the null \emph{and} sits within the ceiling established next.


\section{Results}

\subsection{The ceiling: the group is learnable, the individual is not}
\label{sec:ceiling}

Before asking whether a synthetic audience is accurate, we must ask what accuracy is attainable at all. A one-way random-effects decomposition shows that a single human rating is mostly idiosyncratic: its ICC(1) is $0.470$ on PARA, $0.223$ on EVA, and $0.188$ on LAPIS, so only 19-47\% of a rating is shared with other raters and predictable in principle. The group mean, by contrast, is highly reliable, at ICC(k) $0.958$, $0.906$, and $0.839$ respectively. Persona attributes explain just 1-4\% of \emph{individual} taste, exactly the small value the plurality view predicts, and a collaborative-filtering baseline agrees: knowing who the rater is buys only 0.01-0.03 MAE. This is the quantitative license for everything that follows: aggregation is not a convenience that smooths a noisy predictor, it is where the signal lives, and a method that predicted individual taste well would be suspicious.

\subsection{The synthetic audience is faithful where personas are informative}
\label{sec:audience}

\paragraph{The persona is functional, and calibration is a prerequisite.} The persona moves the judge in the direction the data says it should: at the score level the effect is clearly positive on the attribute-rich datasets ($r{=}0.37$ on PARA, $0.39$ on LAPIS), and it steers the free-text \emph{rationale} far above chance on all three, so a rater's attribute is recoverable from the text at AUC 0.58-0.70 against $\approx$0.50 for the persona-blind control, with four times as many distinct rationales. On EVA that signal is strong in the text even though the integer score hides it: greedy decoding can censor a persona effect the model is in fact computing. Calibration does the rest: it roughly halves group error, pushes the aggregate below the population-mean prior everywhere (Figure~\ref{fig:audience}, left), and transfers across datasets.

\paragraph{The panel reproduces real differences between groups.} Our headline predictive result is that the persona panel recovers genuine divergence \emph{between} viewer groups, not just the grand mean. On paintings (LAPIS), pooled between-group separation is $+0.166$ (95\% CI $[0.150, 0.181]$) against a persona-blind control at $+0.016$ (Figure~\ref{fig:audience}, middle), and every constituent slice (nationality $+0.068$, art interest $+0.088$, age $+0.102$) is positive with an interval excluding zero. Six of eight slice tests survive Holm correction, and every separation is unchanged between greedy and temperature-0.7 decoding. The cross-dataset pattern is interpretable: persona signal scales with persona richness, LAPIS $>$ PARA $>$ EVA. It is strong on paintings, weak but real on PARA ($+0.044$, interval excluding zero), and fails on EVA ($-0.084$), whose descriptions are thinnest. This is a failure we highlight rather than hide, because falling exactly where the richness ordering predicts is itself evidence that the persona, not some confound, is doing the work.

\paragraph{The aggregation mechanism transfers to AI-generated images.} On 23{,}445 crowd votes over 898 generated pairs, the frozen judge role-plays each voter's country and picks the better image. Aggregating recovers the crowd's preference: panel-versus-crowd correlation is $+0.466$ (95\% CI $[0.415, 0.515]$) and accuracy reaches 0.659 against an always-majority prior of 0.600 ($+0.056$, interval excluding zero), while a single simulated vote is near chance at 0.532. Most importantly, accuracy rises monotonically with panel size, 0.588 at $N{=}1$ to 0.657 at $N{=}20$ (Figure~\ref{fig:audience}, right). This is the ceiling argument made visible: a pairwise task, diverse country personas, and temperature-0.7 sampling give the panel the spread that averaging converts into accuracy. On real images that curve is flat, because greedy decoding collapses the personas to near-identical scores, so the group win there comes from image quality plus calibration rather than persona averaging. The matching negative: \emph{between-country} differentiation does not transfer ($+0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.017, 0.020]$, against $+0.17$ on paintings). That follows the very logic that makes aggregation work, since the panel recovers the shared, crowd-level preference even where the persona encodes no real between-group structure, and the test is under-powered at roughly one vote per country-pair cell.

\subsection{AutoPolish: a synthetic audience can steer image editing}
\label{sec:c4}

We run AutoPolish on 100 images (50 PARA, 50 EVA) across four critics and ten steps. The premise validates cleanly (Table~\ref{tab:c4}): a frozen synthetic-audience critic drives a non-circular, identity-preserving loop that measurably improves a \emph{held-out} objective. Every condition's gain interval excludes zero, with society moving the mean aesthetic from 5.10 to 5.27; committed edits end at identity $0.95$-$0.97$, far above the $0.78$ cap; refinement converges by step 3-4; and the oracle behaves as a clean ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:c4quant}). The society critic also gives $3.1\times$ richer feedback than the blind critic (3.12 versus 1.00 distinct complaints per step) and proposes semantically meaningful changes that a fixed string never suggests (Figure~\ref{fig:c4qual}). Read step by step, the loop is legible: complaints accumulate, the anchored re-edit applies them to the original image, and the committed best improves in visible increments while the picture stays itself (Figure~\ref{fig:c4prog}).

We scope the accompanying negative precisely. On this \emph{generic} aesthetic objective the three critic conditions are statistically indistinguishable (society minus blind is $+0.015$, CI $[-0.010, 0.044]$). The likely reason is a measurement ceiling rather than an equivalence of critics: a universal aesthetic rewards generically-prettier edits that almost any instruction achieves (even the oracle reaches only $+0.187$), so it cannot resolve \emph{audience-specific} critique quality. Isolating a society-over-blind advantage needs an evaluator that scores each edit by the target panel's own predicted preference, held out from the proposer, and AutoPolish is built to run exactly that experiment. Its contribution here is the loop itself: non-circular, identity-preserving, audience-driven, with richer feedback and real gains.

\section{Conclusion}

AutoPolish is ultimately a question about \emph{agency}: who decides whether an image is good, and how much of that judgment a creator delegates. It replaces no one's judgment with a scalar verdict; it lets a creator ask ``how would this land with this audience?'' and receive legible feedback they stay free to reject. The audience has a voice; the creator keeps the decision. Simulated group reactions are \emph{dataset-sampled distributions, not demographic essences}, and the model's own national bias is measured and netted out rather than passed along, so the responsible use is ideation in a creator's hands, not automated gatekeeping of taste.

Treating taste as plural is where the signal comes from. Separating the learnable group from the unlearnable individual makes a frozen VLM panel a faithful predictor of group reactions, and AutoPolish turns that predictor into an autonomous, non-circular critic that improves images and says, in words, why.

\section{Limitations and honest negatives}
\label{sec:limits}

We report our negatives in full; each sharpens the claim and points to a next experiment. \textbf{(1) The panel-size curve is flat on real images.} Greedy decoding collapses the panel and temperature-0.7 did not fix it: the backbone reverts to the mode about 88\% of the time on discrete rating tasks. The mechanism is nonetheless clean on generated images and every separation is identical across temperatures, so the headline is no artifact; the fix, reading the token-level score distribution in one forward pass, is designed and parked. \textbf{(2) Cross-cultural differentiation on generated images is null,} under-powered at roughly one vote per cell and limited to a single generator pairing; a powered re-run with a persona-blind control is specified. \textbf{(3) The critic ablation is null on a generic objective,} which cannot resolve audience-specific critique quality; a group-preference evaluator is the fix. \textbf{(4) Thin personas break persona claims,} as EVA shows throughout. \textbf{(5) The judge carries a national rating bias.} A subgroup audit \citep{aesbiasbench2025} finds it fair across personality, age, gender, and expertise (gaps at most 0.04) but biased by nationality (0.50) and region (0.23), an ethics headline and a confound, which is why the generated-image analysis carries global-preference and persona-blind controls that net it out. Leakage, by contrast, is clean: artist fame correlates with error at only $+0.03$, the parse rate is $\approx$100\%, and the zero-shot pipeline uses no AVA exemplars, so the EVA-in-AVA overlap opens no leakage path. None undercuts the core claims: the ceiling, between-group recovery on paintings, aggregation transfer, and the validated AutoPolish loop.


\section{Future directions}
\label{sup:future}
Three extensions follow directly from the results above. Each preserves the distinction that organizes the paper, that the group is learnable and the individual is not, and each is stated as a falsifiable test rather than an aspiration.

\paragraph{Across model families and sizes: is the audience a property of the method or of one backbone?} Every number in this paper comes from a single frozen judge, \texttt{Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct}. That establishes the mechanism exists; it does not establish that it belongs to persona-conditioned aggregation rather than to one model's idiosyncrasies. The clean experiment holds everything else fixed (persona cards, prompts, calibration protocol, folds, seeds, bootstrap) and swaps only the judge, sweeping both \emph{families} (a second open multimodal family, and a hosted frozen model as an upper reference) and \emph{sizes} within one family, so that family and capacity are separable rather than confounded. Three predictions make the sweep falsifiable. First, the persona-richness ordering LAPIS $>$ PARA $>$ EVA should survive every backbone, because it is a property of how richly the datasets describe their viewers and not of the model reading those descriptions; a backbone that reorders it would mean our richness explanation is wrong. Second, between-group separation should rise with scale and instruction-following, since the persona has to be both attended to and expressible in the output. Third, the central-tendency bias should change in magnitude but not vanish, which would confirm that post-hoc calibration is a structural prerequisite of prompted rating rather than a patch for one model. A valuable secondary readout is whether any backbone dissolves limitation~(1) of Section~\ref{sec:limits}: the flat panel-size curve on real images comes from greedy decoding reverting to the modal score about 88\% of the time, so a backbone with a genuinely broader score distribution, or the token-level score readout we describe there, would give aggregation the spread it needs to show itself on real images as it already does on generated ones. If the ordering holds and the panel-size curves rise across families, the synthetic audience is a property of the method; if it holds only for Qwen, the honest conclusion is a backbone effect, and we would report it as one.

\paragraph{Beyond images: audio, text, and mixed panels.} Nothing in the method is specifically visual. A modality needs only three ingredients to carry the pipeline: a corpus of stimuli rated by \emph{identifiable} annotators, so persona cards are built from real attributes and whole users can be held out cold-start; a frozen model that accepts that modality; and, for the editing loop, a held-out grader from a different model family plus an editor that can be anchored to the original artifact. For \textbf{audio}, music and sound-aesthetics corpora with per-listener metadata supply the first ingredient: the panel reacts to a clip, aggregation predicts the listener group's response, and the AutoPolish analogue distills the panel's complaints into one mixing or arrangement instruction applied to the original take, graded by a held-out audio-preference model with a similarity check standing in for the identity constraint. For \textbf{text}, stories and essays with annotator demographics play the same role, with a rewriter as the editor and a held-out preference model as the grader; here the identity constraint becomes semantic: the revision must not change what the piece says. In each case the first study to run is the ceiling analysis rather than the headline: ICC(1) against ICC($k$), and the persona-$\Delta R^2$, say before any modeling whether a group signal exists and how much of the individual is reachable at all. We expect the qualitative pattern to repeat, with individuals near noise and group means reliable, and the persona-richness dependence to bind harder in text, where a reader's background plausibly explains more of the variance than it does for a photograph. A further step is \emph{mixed panels}, where a persona reacts to an image with its caption or a video with its soundtrack; the open question is whether group agreement rises when a modality is added, or whether each added channel supplies one more thing for viewers to disagree about.

\paragraph{Beyond frozen: per-persona adaptation with LoRA.} Keeping every model frozen is what makes this method cheap, reproducible, and non-circular, but it also caps how much of a viewer can be represented: the persona reaches the model only as text in the context window. The natural next step moves part of the persona into \emph{parameters}, fitting a low-rank adapter \citep{hu2022lora} on the frozen backbone so that a viewer's behavior is learned rather than prompted. The design question is what one adapter should stand for, and our own ceiling result answers it. With ICC(1) between 0.19 and 0.47 and a prompted persona effect worth only 1-4\% of individual $R^2$, an adapter fit to a single annotator would mostly fit noise. The better unit is a \emph{bank of attribute adapters}, one per persona attribute or attribute cluster (art expertise, high openness, a nationality group), composed at inference into whichever card the panel calls for. This keeps the training set per adapter large, preserves the compositional structure of the cards, and, unlike a per-user model, targets exactly the level at which the signal was shown to live. The evaluation must be the same three-part one used here: individual-level $R^2$ against both the 1-4\% prompted baseline and the ICC(1) ceiling; between-group separation against the persona-blind control; and whether the isotonic calibration map still transfers across datasets, which is the property a trained model is most likely to break. Two hazards deserve stating in advance. \textbf{Leakage:} an adapter trained on the rating pool it is evaluated on would void the cold-start claim, so user-disjoint and image-disjoint folds stop being a control and become a requirement. \textbf{Circularity:} a learned critic sits closer in distribution to the objective it optimizes against, so the critic $\neq$ objective separation that makes AutoPolish credible would have to be re-measured rather than inherited. The honest framing is a trade. Adaptation buys fidelity to individuals at the price of the frozen claim, and it is worth paying only if it lifts individual-level prediction appreciably above what prompting already reaches, which the ceiling analysis says is a narrow target, and which is precisely why it is worth measuring.

\section{Prompts}
\label{sup:prompts}
Every judgment comes from a frozen VLM under one of the system/user prompt pairs below; no weights are trained. The persona judge (the synthetic audience) and the persona-blind control differ only in the framing shown here. Inside the editing loop, a distiller prompt turns the panel's accumulated complaints into a single imperative instruction.

\paragraph{Persona judge (synthetic audience).} The free-text persona card is inserted verbatim at \pt{\{description\}}.
\begin{promptbox}{System prompt}
\ttfamily\footnotesize
You are role-playing as a person described as follows: \{description\}\\[4pt]
You are shown an image, as if it appeared in your social media feed. React the way this specific person actually would: let their personality, background, values, and taste -- not generic politeness -- decide whether they love it, are indifferent, or dislike it. Not everything deserves a high score; be honest and critical when the image doesn't match this person's taste. Do not mention that you are an AI or that you are role-playing.
\end{promptbox}
\begin{promptbox}{User prompt}
\ttfamily\footnotesize
As this exact person, look at this image. Give it an overall aesthetic score from 0 to 100 for how much YOU personally like it, then name the SINGLE change that would most improve it for your taste. Respond with ONLY a JSON object and nothing else, in exactly this form:\\[2pt]
\{"score": <integer 0-100>, "comment": "<one concrete improvement, imperative>"\}
\end{promptbox}

\paragraph{Persona-blind control (the baseline to beat).}
\begin{promptbox}{System prompt}
\ttfamily\footnotesize
You are an impartial, general-purpose judge of photographic aesthetics.\\[4pt]
You are shown one photograph. Judge it on its own merits the way a typical viewer would, without adopting any particular person's perspective or taste. Not every photo deserves a good score; be honest when a photo is mediocre or disappointing. Do not mention that you are an AI.
\end{promptbox}
\begin{promptbox}{User prompt}
\ttfamily\footnotesize
Look at this image. Give it an overall aesthetic score from 0 to 100, then name the SINGLE most important change that would most improve it. Respond with ONLY a JSON object and nothing else, in exactly this form:\\[2pt]
\{"score": <integer 0-100>, "comment": "<one concrete improvement, imperative>"\}
\end{promptbox}

\paragraph{Distiller (complaints $\to$ one edit instruction).} The running instruction is fed back so complaints accumulate across steps.
\begin{promptbox}{System prompt}
\ttfamily\footnotesize
You are a photo-editing assistant. You convert viewer feedback into ONE concrete, imperative image-edit instruction. Output only the instruction -- no preamble, no quotes, no explanation.
\end{promptbox}
\begin{promptbox}{User prompt}
\ttfamily\footnotesize
[The current edit instruction is: "<accumulated>".]\\[2pt]
Viewers looking at this image want these changes:\\
- <complaint 1>\\
- <complaint 2>\\
\ \ \dots\\[2pt]
Rewrite everything into ONE concrete imperative photo-edit instruction of at most 15 words that an image editor can apply. Output only the instruction.
\end{promptbox}

\section{Example: a panel reacting to one image}
\label{sup:panel-example}
To make the synthetic audience concrete, Figure~\ref{fig:ex-edit} (left) shows one office photograph (an open-plan office, initial held-out aesthetic 5.57), and the boxes below give five of the ten panelists' \emph{actual} reactions to it, produced by the persona prompt above (each panelist returns a score out of 100 and the single change they would most want). The panel does not agree on one thing: the two older, less art-experienced panelists ask for warmer, more natural lighting, while the others ask for plants or artwork to personalize the desks.

\begin{personabox}{Panelist~1: male, 26 to 29, senior high school, art: competent \quad(score 60/100)}
\small ``The lighting could be more natural to make the space feel more inviting.''
\end{personabox}
\begin{personabox}{Panelist~5: male, 35 to 40, senior high school, art: expert \quad(score 60/100)}
\small ``The lighting could be more natural to enhance the warmth and comfort of the space.''
\end{personabox}
\begin{personabox}{Panelist~3: female, 30 to 34, university, art: proficient \quad(score 60/100)}
\small ``Add some plants or artwork to the desks to add some life and personality to the space.''
\end{personabox}
\begin{personabox}{Panelist~6: male, 35 to 40, junior college, art: beginner \quad(score 60/100)}
\small ``Add some plants or artwork to the desks to make the space feel more welcoming and lively.''
\end{personabox}
\begin{personabox}{Panelist~9: female, 18 to 21, junior college, art: beginner \quad(score 60/100)}
\small ``Add some plants or artwork to the desks to make the space feel more welcoming and personal.''
\end{personabox}

\section{Example: from panel feedback to an edit instruction}
\label{sup:aggregation}
The loop collects the panel's ten complaints, distills them into one imperative instruction with the distiller prompt above, applies it to the \emph{original} image with the editor, and commits the result only if the held-out judge improves. For the office image the panel's two themes, lighting and personalization, are merged into a single instruction, and the committed edit adds warmer light, potted plants, and framed artwork, raising the held-out aesthetic from 5.57 to 5.96 (Figure~\ref{fig:ex-edit}).

\begin{instrbox}{Aggregated panel complaints (10 panelists)}
\small
$\bullet$~more natural, warmer lighting to make the space inviting \hfill (2 panelists)\\
$\bullet$~add plants or artwork to the desks to add life and personality \hfill (8 panelists)
\end{instrbox}
\begin{instrbox}{Distilled edit instruction ($\leq 15$ words)}
\small\itshape
Add natural lighting and plants/artwork to the desks to enhance warmth, comfort, and personalization.
\end{instrbox}
